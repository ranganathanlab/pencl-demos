Generalization-Preserving Fine-tuning of BioM3 · Didactic Note 16

Rebuilding the corpus: from format memorization to vocabulary invariance

PenCL was trained on ~45 million text-protein pairs in a single annotation format. The Stage 3 fine-tuning experiments proved that the resulting embedding geometry is the bottleneck for natural language protein design. This note describes the construction of a ~50 million pair corpus with format-diverse and vocabulary-diverse text — the data foundation for PenCL retraining.

Ranganathan Lab · Center for Physics of Evolving Systems · University of Chicago BioM3 ProteoScribe · PenCL (Stage 1) Retraining · April 2026

Section 1 · The bottleneck

PenCL's embedding geometry determines what Stage 3 can learn

The three-stage BioM3 pipeline — PenCL (contrastive alignment), Facilitator (embedding refinement), ProteoScribe (sequence generation) — is trained in stages: Stages 1 and 2 are frozen during Stage 3 fine-tuning. When a user writes a text prompt, PenCL maps it to a 512-dimensional conditioning vector zc that guides ProteoScribe's generation. The quality of generated sequences depends entirely on where in zc space the prompt lands.

PenCL was trained exclusively on SwissProt-formatted text: field-prefixed annotations in a uniform syntactic pattern ("PROTEIN NAME: ... FUNCTION: ... LINEAGE: ..."). PubMedBERT's final transformer blocks were fine-tuned on this format. The result: text containing SwissProt vocabulary and syntax maps to protein-informative zc vectors. Text in any other format or vocabulary maps elsewhere — to regions that carry less information about protein identity.

The C7 fine-tuning experiments proved this directly. At epoch 69, annotation dropout + LoRA + prior preservation created a transient window where all four prompt formats (SwissProt, SGD-style, database-keyword natural language, conversational English) produced SH3 domains with comparable sector conservation — a P1–P-Novel gap of just 9.6%. By epoch 209, the gap had expanded to 61.1%. Stage 3 specialization progressively narrowed the model's responsive zc region toward the zone where PenCL maps training-style text, excluding everything else.

The vocabulary insight. The critical distinction is not format (field prefixes vs. prose) but vocabulary. P5, which uses natural language syntax but SwissProt keywords ("SH3 domain", "HOG pathways", "Cdc42p"), achieves 58.3% sector with the C0 baseline — because PenCL recognizes those keywords regardless of surrounding syntax. P-Novel, which uses conversational vocabulary ("signaling domain", "activate osmotic stress responses"), fails at 29.6%. The corpus must include vocabulary-diverse text, not just format-diverse text.

The component decomposition from C1 (dropout only, through epoch 169) confirms the architecture of the problem. Dropout enables P5: C1 P5 peaks at 65.0% at epoch 119 — the best P5 result from any condition. But without LoRA the window closes (P5 decays to 59.4% by epoch 169), and without prior preservation P-Novel never produces SH3 domains (147 aa, 39.8% sector at epoch 169). Each component addresses a different failure mode, but all three together only create a transient fix. The permanent fix requires changing where PenCL maps text — retraining Stage 1 itself.

Section 2 · The corpus

~50 million text-protein pairs spanning the full format and vocabulary spectrum

The retrained PenCL will see the same protein described in multiple ways: the original SwissProt annotation, partial annotations with fields randomly dropped, free-form scientific English, and minimal name-only descriptions. Over training, the contrastive loss (LGC) pulls all these descriptions toward the same protein embedding, teaching PubMedBERT's final layers to extract biological meaning regardless of how it is expressed.

The corpus has two sides. The Pfam side (~44.8M sequences) provides evolutionary breadth — the LPFC loss learns family relationships from these. The SwissProt side (~570K sequences) provides annotation depth — the text diversity tiers are built from these. Improving the text quality on both sides, and adding format diversity to the SwissProt side, is the core work.

Tiers 2–4 are applied only to the 570K SwissProt proteins, which have rich annotations worth diversifying. The 44.8M Pfam entries are not duplicated — their row count is unchanged — but their text quality improved substantially. Before enrichment, most Pfam entries had generic text: "Protein name: A0A0G2K1R2. Family description: SH3 domain." After enrichment, 68% carry transferred SwissProt annotations with specific protein names, functions, and catalytic activities; 75% have correct organism-specific lineages; and the 14.3M entries that could not be enriched by BLAST were at least rebuilt into proper field-prefixed format.

Additionally, stochastic annotation dropout is applied to all entries during training — including the Pfam entries. Each protein-text pair is presented with a different random field subset in every batch. A single SwissProt protein with 5 Tier 2 variants, 2 Tier 3 descriptions, and 1 Tier 4 description, each seen with different dropout masks across 10 epochs, produces hundreds of distinct text inputs that the contrastive loss pulls toward the same protein embedding.

Section 3 · Track A: Pfam enrichment

30.5 million Pfam sequences gain specific, informative annotations

The original Pfam text is formulaic and repetitive: "Major Facilitator Superfamily. Family description: The major facilitator superfamily. GENE ONTOLOGY: transmembrane transport." Many thousands of sequences in the same family share identical text. This gives PenCL almost no information to distinguish one MFS transporter from another — the text side of the contrastive loss cannot learn meaningful protein-text alignment when the text is the same for every family member.

Track A uses MMseqs2 to search all 44.8M Pfam sequences against the 569K SwissProt entries and transfers the closest homolog's full annotation. A Pfam sequence that was paired with a generic family description now gets the SwissProt hit's PROTEIN NAME, FUNCTION, LINEAGE, CATALYTIC ACTIVITY, and other fields — specific, informative text that describes what the protein actually does.

Section 4 · Phase 2: Multi-database enrichment

Every sequence needs its own lineage; every enzyme needs its EC number

Track A transferred SwissProt annotations as monolithic text blobs. Phase 2 goes further: it queries the individual annotation databases to add specific fields that the BLAST transfer may have missed or that apply to all 44.8M entries, not just the 30.5M with hits.

The most important addition is LINEAGE. In the current PenCL training data, most Pfam entries have no organism-specific lineage. But LINEAGE is the field that varies per sequence and drives phylogenetic conditioning in ProteoScribe — it is what allows the model to generate a bacterial version vs. a fungal version of the same fold. Phase 2 maps every Pfam sequence to its source organism via the UniProt ID mapping database, then looks up the full NCBI taxonomy lineage. The target is near-100% LINEAGE coverage.

The remaining databases add family-level annotations that apply uniformly to all members of a Pfam family:

Section 5 · Track C: Dropout variants and minimal descriptions

3.42 million mechanical text variants from the 570K SwissProt proteins

Tier 2: Annotation dropout variants

For each SwissProt protein, 5 dropout variants are pre-computed by randomly retaining or dropping each annotation field according to a tiered retention schedule. The schedule reflects the information hierarchy: fields that define protein identity (name, function, lineage) are retained most often; fields that specify subfamily (paralog, catalytic activity, pathway) are retained moderately; supplementary fields (subunit, localization, tissue specificity) are retained least often.

TierFieldsRetentionRationale
CorePROTEIN NAME100%Never dropped — anchors protein identity
CoreFUNCTION, LINEAGE85%What it does + where it evolved
SpecifyingPARALOG NAME, CATALYTIC ACTIVITY, FAMILY NAMES65%Narrows to subfamily or enzymatic class
SpecifyingPATHWAY, DOMAIN55%Functional context and domain architecture
SupplementarySUBUNIT, SUBCELLULAR LOCATION, SIMILARITY, PTM, COFACTOR, TISSUE SPECIFICITY, and all remaining fields40%Optional context — model learns these are dispensable

The schedule is stored as a JSON configuration file and can be adjusted before PenCL training starts. Each variant uses a deterministic seed derived from the protein accession and variant index, so the dropout is reproducible. PROTEIN NAME is never dropped, ensuring every variant carries at least the fold identity.

An example: a SwissProt entry for Sho1 with 8 annotation fields might produce variants ranging from the full annotation minus SUBUNIT and SIMILARITY (6 fields retained) down to just PROTEIN NAME and LINEAGE (core fields only). Across 5 variants, PenCL sees the same protein described at different levels of detail.

Tier 4: Minimal descriptions

One minimal description per protein, constructed mechanically from the protein name, family, and top-level taxonomy. These are plain English — no field prefixes, no database syntax. Templates are randomly selected per protein:

"A SH3 domain protein."

"A chorismate mutase from bacteria."

"A serine protease, trypsin family, found in vertebrates."

"An ABC transporter from fungi."

These test the extreme of the format spectrum: can PenCL learn that "A SH3 domain protein" and "PROTEIN NAME: SH3 domain. FUNCTION: Plasma membrane osmosensor. LINEAGE: ..." describe overlapping identities? The contrastive loss must pull the minimal description toward the same protein embedding as the full annotation.

Section 6 · Track B: LLM-generated natural language descriptions

The vocabulary constraint is the scientific crux of Tier 3

Tier 3 generates 2 free-form scientific descriptions per SwissProt protein using the Claude API. These descriptions must use genuinely different vocabulary from the database annotations — not just different syntax. This is the most important tier and the hardest to get right.

The vocabulary insight means that a description using the same keywords as SwissProt ("SH3 domain", "osmosensor", "HOG pathway") teaches PenCL nothing new — those keywords already map to protein-informative zc vectors. The value of Tier 3 is in bringing new vocabulary into the training distribution: scientific terms, mechanistic descriptions, and functional language that a biophysicist would use but that never appear in SwissProt.

BAD: Same keywords, different format

"This is an SH3 domain protein that functions as a plasma membrane osmosensor for activation of the HOG pathway."

Still uses "SH3 domain", "osmosensor", "HOG pathway" — the SwissProt keywords PenCL already knows.

GOOD: Genuinely different vocabulary

"A yeast transmembrane signaling adaptor whose proline-recognition module detects changes in external solute concentration and recruits a MAP kinase kinase to initiate glycerol accumulation."

"Proline-recognition module" for SH3, "external solute concentration" for osmotic stress. PenCL must learn these are equivalent.

The prompt template instructs Claude to rephrase every technical term — protein names into structural/functional descriptions, pathway names into mechanistic descriptions, organism names into common language. Two descriptions per protein with different emphasis: one focused on function and mechanism, the other on evolutionary context and structural features.

Two tiers of description quality

Tier 3a (all 569K proteins): Database-informed synthesis. Claude reads the SwissProt annotation and writes descriptions using its own scientific vocabulary. This covers the entire corpus.

Tier 3b (well-characterized subset, ~20–50K proteins): Literature-informed synthesis. For proteins with substantial published literature (≥10 PubMed references), Claude also draws on abstracts from key publications. The descriptions capture structure-function reasoning, evolutionary logic, and mechanistic understanding — the kind of language a biophysicist would use in a review article. This is a deeper intervention that brings genuinely novel vocabulary into the corpus.

Quality control. 100 test samples are generated before committing to the full 569K. The test samples are reviewed for: genuine vocabulary diversity (not just reformatted keywords), biological accuracy, appropriate length (2–4 sentences), and absence of database syntax. The prompt template will likely require 1–2 iterations.

Section 7 · What the LLM adds

Instructing a language model to redescribe 569,000 proteins in its own scientific vocabulary

Tier 3 descriptions are generated by Claude Sonnet 4.6 (Anthropic), a general-purpose language model with broad training across the scientific literature. The choice of model reflects two constraints. First, the model must have deep enough knowledge of protein biology, structural biochemistry, and evolutionary biology to rephrase SwissProt annotations in genuinely different vocabulary — not just rearrange the same keywords. A model trained only on database text would reproduce database vocabulary. A model trained on the broader scientific literature can draw on textbook descriptions, review articles, and mechanistic language that never appears in SwissProt. Second, the model must be cost-efficient at scale: 569,000 proteins × 2 descriptions ≈ 1.14 million API calls, processed via the Anthropic batch API at 50% discount. Sonnet 4.6 ($1.50/$7.50 per million tokens at batch pricing) provides the right balance of scientific depth and cost.

The prompt design is where the vocabulary constraint is enforced. The system prompt instructs the model to rephrase every technical term — protein names into structural or functional descriptions, pathway names into mechanistic descriptions, organism names into plain language — and to write in the kind of scientific English a biophysicist would use in a review article. Two descriptions are requested per protein: one emphasizing function and mechanism, the other emphasizing evolutionary context and structural features. This ensures that PenCL sees each protein described with two distinct vocabularies, maximizing the diversity of zt vectors that the contrastive loss must pull together.

You are a protein biologist writing descriptions for a colleague who does not use database conventions. Your task: describe the protein in your own scientific language. You MUST rephrase every technical term and concept — do not copy phrases from the input. Use mechanistic and physicochemical language where possible.

Rules:
— NO database field prefixes (no "PROTEIN NAME:", "FUNCTION:", etc.)
— NO database identifiers (no accession numbers, PF IDs, GO terms)
— Rephrase protein names into structural/functional descriptions
— Rephrase pathway names into mechanistic descriptions
— Each description: 2–4 sentences of natural scientific English
— Include organism context, function, and any structural or biophysical detail
Here is the database annotation for this protein:

{text_caption}

Write two descriptions of this protein, each using different vocabulary and emphasis. Separate them with |||.

Description 1 should emphasize function and mechanism.
Description 2 should emphasize evolutionary context and structural features.

The critical test is whether this prompt produces genuinely different vocabulary rather than reformatted keywords. 100 test proteins were generated and reviewed before committing to the full corpus. The test confirmed: zero database field prefixes, zero accession numbers, 0.19% residual keyword overlap, and consistent hedging on uncharacterized proteins (zero hallucinated functions across 10 uncharacterized entries). For uncharacterized proteins, the LLM does not hallucinate function. What PenCL learns from these descriptions. The contrastive loss pulls the SwissProt annotation's zt vector and the Tier 3 description's zt vector toward the same protein embedding. After training, PubMedBERT must map "glucose transporter" and "facilitative monosaccharide uniporter" to similar zt vectors — because they describe the same protein. "Polyketide synthase" and "assembly-line enzyme" must converge. "SH3 domain" and "proline-recognition module" must converge. This is how vocabulary invariance is built into the embedding geometry.

Section 8 · Training protocol

Option A: format invariance from data diversity alone

The training protocol is deliberately conservative. No changes to the PenCL loss function (LGC + LPFC + LbML + LpML). No changes to the architecture (ESM2 650M + PubMedBERT 100M + projection heads). No changes to the joint embedding dimension (512). The only intervention is the training data and the per-batch dropout.

The key mechanism: for each SwissProt protein in a batch, one text representation is sampled from a random tier (original, dropout variant, natural language, or minimal). Over training, the contrastive loss sees the same protein paired with all its text variants across different epochs. PubMedBERT's final transformer blocks adapt to extract protein-informative features from any text format — not just SwissProt syntax. The MLM loss (LbML) simultaneously trains PubMedBERT to understand biomedical language in diverse styles.

This is Option A from the literature analysis: multi-view contrastive learning through data diversity. No explicit text-text contrastive term. No modifications to the projection heads or embedding dimension. The theoretical precedent is strong — SimCSE showed that dropout alone creates effective contrastive pairs; COSMOS showed that text cropping during contrastive training improves zero-shot performance; LAION-Audio showed that keyword-to-caption augmentation improves audio-text alignment. Our annotation dropout is a domain-specific, biologically principled version of these techniques.

Section 9 · Validation cascade

Two stages of validation, one definitive success criterion

Stage 1: Embedding space validation (after PenCL retraining)

Before retraining the Facilitator or ProteoScribe, validate that the embedding space has the right geometry. For 1,000 proteins, compute zt embeddings from all text tiers and measure cosine similarity between tiers for the same protein. The retrained PenCL should map "PROTEIN NAME: SH3 domain. FUNCTION: Plasma membrane osmosensor..." and "A yeast transmembrane signaling adaptor whose proline-recognition module detects changes in external solute concentration..." to zt vectors with cosine similarity >0.8. The current PenCL achieves ~0.3–0.5.

Simultaneously, proteins from different families must remain well-separated. The retrained PenCL must achieve clustering metrics (CHI, DBI) comparable to or better than the original, and standard benchmarks (zero-shot subcellular localization, remote homology detection) must not degrade.

Stage 3: Fine-tuning validation (the definitive test)

Fine-tune ProteoScribe on SH3 142K with C7-style annotation dropout, using the retrained PenCL embeddings. Evaluate at epochs 49, 69, 99, 129, and 149 with all four prompts.

The success criterion is the P1–P-Novel gap at late epochs. Currently this gap explodes from 9.6% at epoch 69 to 47% at epoch 149 because PenCL maps P-Novel vocabulary to a zc region that Stage 3 excludes as it specializes. With retrained PenCL, all prompt vocabularies occupy the same zc region, so there is no region to exclude. The gap should remain below 15% at epoch 149+.
